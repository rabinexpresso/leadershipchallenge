### Scenario 0 — The Pivot
**Situation (new):** Management just dropped a message in Slack saying the team is shifting priorities. No explanation, no context — just "we're shifting gears." Your team has been heads-down on a project for three weeks. They have no idea this is coming. You're about to walk into the office.

**A (new):** Pull the team together now. Tell them what you know — even if it's not much — and work through it with them.
**B (new):** Sit on it for now. Pull one or two trusted people in quietly, get a clearer picture, then speak to the full team.
**C (new):** Go up to your manager first. Get the full story before you say a word to your team.

### Scenario 1 — The Mirror
**Situation (new):** Your manager pulls you aside after a meeting. They tell you — kindly, but clearly — that some people on your team find it hard to bring problems to you. That you can come across as dismissive when things go wrong. You had no idea. You're caught off guard.

**A (new):** Ask your manager who said it. Then go directly to those people this week to hear their side.
**B (new):** Take it seriously. Go back to your team this week — not to explain yourself, but to genuinely ask what you could do better.
**C (new):** Acknowledge it professionally. You're not sure it's fully accurate, but you'll be more mindful going forward.

### Scenario 2 — The Handover
**Situation (new):** You're handing over a core process you've spent 18 months building to a new team lead. You know every edge case, every workaround, every thing that breaks. They're smart — but they're completely new to this.

**A (new):** Build them a thorough document — every step, every exception, every common mistake. Then walk them through it together.
**B (new):** Start with the *why* before the *how*. Let them ask questions, own the messy parts, and figure some of it out themselves.
**C (new):** Give them a list of the top ten mistakes to avoid. Then completely step back and let them run.

### Scenario 3 — The Change You Did Not Choose
**Situation (new):** A change is rolling out company-wide. Your team didn't ask for it and isn't happy about it. Honestly — you have your own doubts too, but you haven't told anyone.

**A (new):** Get behind it fully. Bring it to your team with confidence and keep your reservations to yourself.
**B (new):** Be honest with your team — tell them you have questions too, but that you're going to work through it together. Let them have input on how it rolls out.
**C (new):** Champion it fully to your team. Take your questions to your manager privately.

### Scenario 4 — Monday Morning
**Situation (new):** It's 8:45am Monday. You've got three things staring at you: a team member needs an urgent HR conversation, a client issue needs your sign-off, and your manager wants a project update in 90 minutes. You can't do all three.

**A (new):** Handle the client issue first — it'll be quick, and then you can give the HR conversation the attention it deserves.
**B (new):** Delegate the client issue to someone capable on your team. Deal with HR first, then prep the update.
**C (new):** Message your manager asking for 30 more minutes. Handle HR first, then delegate the client issue.

### Scenario 5 — The 70% Decision
**Situation (new):** For three weeks you've been watching something in your team's workflow that doesn't feel right. You don't have hard data but your gut is pretty sure what the problem is — and what to do about it. Your manager wants your recommendation in tomorrow's meeting.

**A (new):** Present it as a working hypothesis. Tell your manager you don't have data yet, but here's your reasoning and you're ready to test it immediately.
**B (new):** Back yourself. Make the recommendation clearly, put your name on it, and accept that if you're wrong you'll adjust.
**C (new):** Ask for another week. You want to come with real data, not just a hunch.

### Scenario 6 — The Walkout Risk
**Situation (new):** Your best person just told you they've been offered another job — 20% more than they're on now. They're not threatening to leave. They're telling you because they respect you. You have no power over pay. You've got ten minutes before your next meeting.

### Scenario 7 — The Difficult Conversation
**Situation (new):** Someone on your team has missed three deadlines in a row. Others have noticed and they're quietly frustrated. This week, another team member comes to you directly and says it's starting to affect their own work.

**A (new):** Don't wait for a 1:1. Bring it up the next time you see them. Be specific about what you've observed and what needs to change.
**B (new):** Bring it up gently in your next 1:1. Frame it more as a check-in on how they're doing than a performance conversation.
**C (new):** Start by asking what they need to get back on track — let them lead — then share what you've been observing.

### Scenario 8 — The Approval Loop
**Situation (new):** Your team has been stuck waiting on a decision for six days. Your manager is away and barely reachable. Technically the decision is above your level — but it's not complicated, the delay is hurting team morale, and you know exactly what the right call is.

**A (new):** Make the call. Write down your reasoning, send your manager a note explaining what you did and why, and own whatever happens next.
**B (new):** Write your manager a short message — here's the situation, here's what I think we should do, I'm going to move on this by end of tomorrow if I don't hear back.
**C (new):** Get the most senior person on your team fully up to speed and hand the decision to them.

### Scenario 9 — The Rebuild
**Situation (new):** Two people on your team resigned in the same week. You've still got four people left — all of them stretched and not in great shape. There's a major deadline in three weeks. The resignations weren't completely surprising. It's been a hard few months. But the timing is brutal.

**A (new):** Get everyone together today. Be honest that this is hard. Ask them what they need. Rebuild the workload plan with them in the room.
**B (new):** Triage immediately. Strip everything non-critical. Give the team one clear path to the finish line. Deal with everything else after.
**C (new):** Go to your manager first. Push for a deadline extension or extra resources before you commit to anything with your team.

### Scenario 10 — The Feedback You Did Not Give
**Situation (new):** Three weeks ago, a team member cut off a client on a call and dismissed their concern in a way that was clearly noticed by everyone in the room. You didn't say anything at the time. Life moved on. They have a client call tomorrow. It's still sitting with you.

**A (new):** Bring it up today. Yes, it's three weeks late. Be specific about what happened and why it matters before tomorrow's call.
**B (new):** Let that specific incident go. Watch tomorrow's call closely and give feedback straight after if anything comes up.
**C (new):** Have a broader conversation about client communication today — without bringing up the specific incident.

### Scenario 11 — The Credit Question
**Situation (new):** Your team just delivered a great result on a high-profile project. In the debrief with senior leadership, your manager attributes most of the success to decisions made above your team level. Your team is in the room. They notice. Afterwards, two of them come to you separately.

**A (new):** Say something before the meeting ends. Name your team's specific contributions clearly — even if it means gently correcting what was said.
**B (new):** Let the meeting finish. Then go straight to your manager and tell them what you noticed and why it matters.
**C (new):** Don't escalate it. Make sure every single team member hears directly from you how you saw their contribution.

### New Scenario A — The Rookie Who Can't Seem to Get It Right
*(On-the-job training — Alaya/HLE context)*

**Situation:** You've been mentoring a new loan analyst for two months. She's eager, asks good questions, and genuinely wants to do well. But she keeps making the same kind of error — missing fields in client loan files, submitting incomplete applications that bounce back from the lender. You've mentioned it twice in passing. It just happened again, this time holding up a client's approval by three days. The client called to complain. You were the one who apologised.

**Option A:** Sit down with her properly this week — not in passing. Walk through the specific errors, show her the pattern you've been seeing, and set a clear standard for what "done" looks like before anything gets submitted.

### New Scenario B — The Manager Who Goes Sharp Under Pressure
*(Leading with Aggression — Alaya Compass Trap #2)*

**Situation:** A manager on your team runs one of the more technically capable pods. Their team delivers accurate work and rarely misses deadlines. But a pattern has been building. When things go wrong — a file is rejected, a process is missed — this manager reacts sharply. Last week a team member made an error and the manager responded in the team meeting: "How many times does this need to be explained? This is basic." They send short, cutting messages in the team chat that the whole pod can see. Two analysts from their team have separately found quiet moments to tell you they now avoid flagging problems to their manager because of how they react — which means issues are being hidden rather than surfaced.

The manager's output metrics look fine. But The Compass is clear: using intimidation or hostile language that creates a fearful environment is a direct violation — regardless of what the numbers show. A team that is afraid to raise problems is a team that will eventually produce bigger ones.

**Option A:** Address it directly with the manager this week. Share specifically what you have observed and what you have heard from their team — without naming who came to you. Name it clearly: this behaviour violates Alaya's values and it needs to stop, regardless of how strong their team's output appears.
- Scores: { T:2, P:3, A:1 }

**Option B:** Start with genuine curiosity. Ask the manager how they feel the team dynamic is going and whether they are aware of how they come across when things go wrong. Give them the chance to surface it themselves before you share what you know.
- Scores: { T:3, P:1, E:1 }

**Option C:** Quietly adjust how their team escalates problems — route sensitive issues through you or another senior person for now — while you prepare for a more formal conversation later.
- Scores: { A:1 }

**Discuss prompt:** When someone's results are strong but their behaviour is damaging — how long is it fair to wait before saying something?

**Split prompt:** What did the people who chose differently see in that situation that you did not?

### New Scenario C — The Two Accounts That Don't Match
*(Managerial dilemma — conflicting stories)*

**Situation:** Two loan analysts on your team — both solid performers — came to you separately this week. One says the other took credit for a referral she sourced, which meant the wrong person received the commission. He says the referral came through his own network and her involvement was minimal. You were not in the room when it happened. Both are frustrated. Both are waiting for you to do something. The commission has already been paid out.

**Option A:** Bring both of them into the same room together. Tell them directly: you have two accounts that don't match and you need to understand what actually happened. Ask them to walk through it in front of each other.
- Scores: { T:2, P:2, A:1 }

**Option B:** Speak to each of them separately and thoroughly first — go through emails, call logs, referral records — before drawing any conclusion or saying anything definitive to either of them.
- Scores: { T:1, P:3, A:1 }

**Option C:** Tell both of them honestly that the situation is too unclear to resolve fairly after the fact. Shift the conversation to how referrals get attributed going forward — put a clear, agreed process in place so this cannot happen again.
- Scores: { T:1, E:2, A:3 }

**Discuss prompt:** When you can't find a clear answer — is moving forward without one a leadership decision or an avoidance of one?
